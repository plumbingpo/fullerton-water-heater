Many of the installation calls we get in Fullerton, CA are not replacements at all. A family adds a bathroom and needs more capacity. Someone wants the tank moved from a hall closet to the garage or basement. A remodel calls for a second unit at the far end of the house, or an all-electric conversion means trading gas for a heat pump model. Every one of them involves new work: gas lines that must be sized for the load, vents that must be routed and pitched correctly, circuits that must match the heater, and water lines that must be run cleanly. Fullerton Water Heater works all of that out before the installation starts.

Sizing and placement decide most of the outcome. For a tank, we compare first-hour ratings against your household's busiest hour; for a tankless unit, we size by flow rate and winter temperature rise. Then comes placement. A gas heater needs a vent route and combustion air, a heat pump heater needs open space and a condensate drain, and a whole-house electric tankless unit usually needs a big dedicated circuit and matching panel capacity. Put a heater above a finished ceiling and it needs a drain pan; put a gas unit in a garage and code may require a raised platform. You get the trade-offs in plain terms, with no pressure.

The install itself follows a checklist: gas lines sized, run, and leak-tested with a sediment trap at the heater, venting sized and pitched to the manufacturer's spec, circuits and breakers matched to the heater's load, and water lines connected with shutoffs and dielectric protection where dissimilar metals meet. The relief valve gets a full-size discharge line to a safe point, and we add an expansion tank, drain pan, or seismic straps wherever code calls for them, including the permit when one is required. Before we leave, we fill and purge the tank, check draft and carbon monoxide on gas units, set the temperature near 120 degrees, and walk you through everything. Warning Signs to Watch For

A heater that is starting to struggle rarely hides it for long, and the clues are easy to spot once you know them. These are the red flags our technicians see most often before a breakdown:

  • A water heater installed above finished rooms with no drain pan
  • Big-tub or multi-shower demand the current heater cannot meet
  • A planned switch from gas to electric, or to a heat pump model
  • Missing code items like an expansion tank, drain pan, or proper venting
  • A remodel that moves the utility space to a new spot
  • An addition too far from the existing heater for quick hot water
  • Long waits for hot water at faucets far from the heater
  • Hot water running out before everyone has showered

Why This Job Calls for a Pro

Start with the gas line. An undersized line starves a new heater whenever the furnace or range runs, and a whole-house tankless unit usually needs more gas than the existing line carries. The vent has to be the right material, size, and pitch, with proper clearances, so exhaust leaves the home instead of drifting back into it. Electric models need their own math on breaker, wire gauge, and panel capacity. Add relief valve piping, dielectric fittings, expansion tanks, drain pans, seismic straps, and permit rules that vary from one jurisdiction to the next, and there are a lot of details to get right.

What you get from a pro is an installation that is done once. Every joint is tested, every gas install is checked for draft and carbon monoxide, and the temperature is set to protect against scalding. Simple Ways to Extend Your Water Heater's Life

Routine maintenance is almost always cheaper and easier than an early replacement. Follow these tips from our technicians:

  • Start a flushing routine early, before sediment has a chance to harden
  • Moisture in the drain pan or at the relief line is an early warning worth a call
  • Plan an annual descaling if you installed a tankless unit
  • Schedule regular maintenance, including an anode rod check
  • Leave working space around the heater, and never stack storage against it
  • Store paint, gasoline, and solvents far from any gas water heater
  • Keep the temperature around 120 degrees; hotter settings raise the risk of scalding
